# Simplicity Is in the Eye of the Beholder

"General Purpose Languages" promise to make programming easier by covering every case. What they actually do is force everyone, regardless of what they're building, to adopt the same pile of complexity — instead of letting each of us go looking for the simplicity that actually fits our problem. I don't think that's an accident of language design. I think it's what happens when you forget that "simple" was never one thing to begin with.

# What Is Simplicity?

Ask most engineers what "simple" means and you'll get some version of "easy to understand" or "not complicated." That's circular — it just restates the word. Here's a sharper definition, one that actually does work: **simplicity is a lack of nuance.**

That sounds backwards. We're trained to admire nuance. A "nuanced" argument is a sophisticated one; a nuanced understanding is a deep one. But nuance is also where complexity hides. Every special case, every "well, except when," every extra rule bolted on to handle one more scenario — that's nuance accumulating. A system with a lot of nuance is a system with a lot of surface area for things to go wrong, and a lot of surface area a newcomer has to learn before they can be trusted with it.

But there's a second, harder truth sitting underneath the first one: simplicity isn't a property of the thing itself. It's a property of the thing *relative to what you're trying to do with it*. Simplicity is in the eye of the beholder.

If you're an accountant, a spreadsheet is about as simple as tools get — rows, columns, formulas, done. But if you're trying to analyze a program for self-consistency and correctness, a spreadsheet stops being simple the moment you try to use it for that job. It doesn't have the nuance to represent the edge cases correctness analysis actually needs, so you end up bolting nuance on by hand until it can — at which point it's not the simple tool you started with anymore.

The same split shows up in language design. If you're trying to invent a new software system, garbage collection is a genuine boon — it lets you stop thinking about low-level memory management and put your attention on the design instead, and deep type checking mostly gets in your way while you're still figuring out what you're building. But if you're production-engineering that system afterward — dotting every i, crossing every t, squeezing real efficiency out of the design — the opposite is true. Now you want to get rid of generalized garbage collection in favor of memory management tuned to exactly this project, and you want as much type checking as you can get, because that's what catches the edge cases before they ship. A language that makes deep type checking effortless is "simple" for that job. A language that lets you manipulate memory in excruciating detail, while still checking that every operation is self-consistent and safe, is "simple" for that job too — a different job, a different simplicity.

Physics has a name for this kind of move: much-greater-than (`>>`) analysis. You look at a system, decide which effects dominate in the regime you actually care about, and drop everything that's small by comparison. It's how you get a spherical cow: an idealization that's genuinely useful for the question in front of you, and genuinely wrong the moment you forget it was only ever an idealization of one regime. The discipline isn't in finding the simplification — it's in remembering which domain it's valid for, and knowing that you'll eventually need to superimpose several such simplifications, each valid in its own regime, to get back something closer to the full picture.

I think we've largely forgotten that our dominant notation for programming — the functional one — is exactly this kind of simplification. It was built for a specific regime: analyzing programs, proving things about them, reasoning about what they compute. It was not built for designing them, and it was not built for controlling machines. Most of what we actually mean by "programming," in practice, is writing software that controls a machine — I/O, timing, hardware, concurrency, the physical world leaking in at the edges — and that's a domain the functional simplification was never valid for. We inherited the `>>` analysis, forgot it was one, and started treating a tool for analyzing programs as though it were the definition of what a program is.

# Examples of Simplicity

With that caveat in front of you — that every example below is simple *for a purpose*, not simple in some absolute sense — here are some cases where someone found the edges of a domain and built a notation that fit only inside them.

**UNIX** made this move at the level of the whole operating system. Devices, pipes, sockets, regular data on disk — wildly different things — all get normalized to one kind of object: a file, addressed through a file descriptor. You don't need a special API for talking to a printer versus reading a config versus piping output from one program into another. It's all `read()` and `write()` on a descriptor. That single normalization is why shell pipelines work at all, and it's a textbook case of stripping nuance out of an interface until only one shape is left.

**Forth Haiku** shows the same move at a much smaller scale. A few lines of dense, unglamorous Forth notation can expand into a hundred lines of GLSL shader code. The notation is small because it says exactly what needs to be said and nothing else — the complexity of the output is real, but it isn't paid for in the complexity of the source.

**Forth** itself and **Lisp 1.5** both make this trade at the language-design level: an almost absurdly small set of primitives (words and a stack in Forth's case, s-expressions and a handful of special forms in Lisp's) that compose into everything else. Neither language tries to have a construct for every situation. They have one or two constructs and trust you to build the rest.

**BNF**, and its descendant eBNF, do for grammars what UNIX did for I/O: instead of describing "how to parse this particular language," they give you one small notation for describing *any* string-matching grammar. Once you know BNF, you can read the shape of a language you've never seen before, because the notation for describing structure is separated from the structure itself.

**CPU opcodes** are simplicity imposed on physics. Underneath every instruction is a genuine rat's nest of electronics — gates, latches, timing — but the opcode gives you one normalized unit of action ("add these two registers") instead of asking you to reason about the rat's nest directly. It's the same move UNIX makes with files: pick one small vocabulary and hide the mess behind it.

**Feynman diagrams** did this for particle physics. Before them, interactions were described with sprawling functional equations. Feynman gave physicists a small set of lines and vertices that could be composed like a notation, and the diagrams could be read and manipulated almost visually instead of algebraically. Complicated interactions became combinations of a few simple pictures.

**K&R C**, compared to the ANSI C that came after it, is a smaller and blunter language. It defined every routine as a "function," full stop, and it shipped with a type-checking model that was good enough to build real systems software without trying to be good enough for everything. It didn't cover every case. That was the point — it covered *enough* cases, and refused the temptation to keep adding nuance to cover the rest.

UNIX itself makes the point sharper. It wasn't invented in C — it was invented in assembler, and stayed that way until Version 4 rewrote the kernel in C in 1973. Assembler was "simple enough" for inventing the core of an operating system; K&R C came later, as a step toward production-engineering and extending what the original, simpler constructs had already proven out. Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad tells the same story from computer graphics — also built in assembler, also invented rather than production-engineered. Asked how he'd managed to build the first interactive graphics program, the first non-procedural programming language, and the first object-oriented software system in a single year, Sutherland said, "Well, I didn't know it was hard." Assembler was "simple" for what he was doing — not because it had fewer nuances than a high-level language would have, but because inventing didn't yet need the nuances a high-level language exists to manage.

**Prolog**, and relational programming generally, buys simplicity of expression at a real cost: you describe relationships instead of steps, and the language figures out how to satisfy them, at the cost of runtime performance. Simplicity, here, is a trade of your cognitive load for CPU cycles — not a free lunch, just a different bill.

**Children's LEGO blocks** are maybe the purest physical example. There's exactly one type of connector — a round peg of fixed dimensions — and every block uses it. No block is tightly coupled to any other specific block; anything connects to anything. That single, boring, unglamorous decision is why a five-year-old can build things an engineer would call "extensible" without ever hearing the word.

**Multi-stage computation** — pipelines where each stage does one thing and does it well — applies the same idea to how we structure programs, not just interfaces. When a stage only has to do one job, the person writing it can actually think about that job deeply, instead of splitting their attention across five concerns and reaching for workarounds to keep them from tangling.

# Examples of Anti-Simplicity

The failure mode isn't "trying and failing to be simple." It's usually nuance creeping in disguised as generality — someone reaching to cover every domain at once instead of admitting which one they're actually in.

**Overuse of the functional paradigm** is the clearest case. Forcing every problem into the shape of a mathematical function creates workarounds wherever the problem doesn't naturally fit that shape, and a lot of real problems don't. This isn't an accident of language design; it's a hangover from using CPUs to automate *mathematics* first, and programming second. Most languages are built around "functions" even though the CPUs underneath them don't implement functions at all — you need extra code just to turn a subroutine into something that behaves like one. We inherited a nuance we didn't have to have.

**User-defined types** are another. Machines fundamentally have only a couple of types — a bit, and a pointer to a bit, optimized into bytes for convenience. Every user-defined type is a new layer of invented structure sitting on top of that, and each layer needs its own type-checking rules. It's nuance stacked on nuance, and it's why type systems keep growing more elaborate instead of settling down.

**Using "functions" to describe asynchronous parts** is the sharpest case, because it's a category error dressed up as convenience. A function call is synchronous by nature — the caller blocks and waits for the callee to answer. Asynchronous parts are, definitionally, not that. Describing async behavior with a synchronous vocabulary doesn't just add nuance — it adds nuance that actively lies about how the system behaves.

**BNF**, held up above as a genuine simplification, is also a cautionary tale in how a simplification gets captured by one purpose and then mistaken for having only that purpose. Almost immediately after BNF appeared, its main use became building the same kind of thing over and over: niggly, bottom-up, over-burdened parser state machines that are "simple" only if your job is shipping production-quality parsers. That's a narrow reading of a notation good for a lot more than that — specifying string matchers for programming-tool development, for instance, matching recursive text structures in ways plain regular expressions can't easily reach. PEG — formalized by Bryan Ford in the mid-2000s, with OhmJS a well-known later implementation — finally gave recursive-descent, top-down parsing a first-class alternative to BNF's bottom-up habits, but even PEG is still mostly discussed as competition for production parser generators, not as a door into a different way of building programming tools altogether.

**Operating systems** are the anti-simplicity example that's easiest to miss, because we've stopped being able to imagine programming without one. Step back far enough, though, and the concept isn't actually necessary — early gaming systems are a working proof of that. A game cartridge plugged into hardware that had no operating system on it at all, and none of the complicated, brittle fluff that comes bundled with a modern OS was needed to make that work.

The original intent was reasonable: supply common libraries of code so program development goes faster. But instead of building those libraries the way LEGO blocks are built, joined through one small interchangeable connector instead of welded tightly to each other, we built them inside the functional paradigm, and got a huge ball of over-coupled functionality that ships to every user whether they need 2% of it or 90%, and gets cherry-picked at runtime. Instead of production-engineering individual applications, we kept adding nuance and complication to the libraries themselves, hoping a big enough general-purpose pile of code could solve every production problem in advance. And because the functional paradigm was never suited to expressing asynchronous operation or the composition of programs in the first place, that pile keeps growing without closing the gap — getting closer to the asymptote, never reaching it.

Operating systems genuinely make software development simpler, and that part is worth keeping. Where the reasoning breaks down is the next step: shipping that same sprawling, general-purpose pile whole-hog to every end user's machine. Underneath the megabytes and gigabytes, an operating system is mostly a much more complicated replacement for hardware that used to be a rotary switch or a game cartridge — simpler to use, simpler to reason about, and not carrying around code for every possibility it will never be asked to serve.

GPUs are one production-engineering answer to a simple problem: how do you provide a lump of code for every pixel on a screen? Maybe the simpler answer — in hardware and in software — would have been one small CPU, with its own private memory, per pixel.

# How Do You Teach Simplicity?

I don't know yet.

What I notice, looking back over the examples above, is that they all seem to come from the same place: [first-principles thinking](https://guitarvydas.github.io/2022/05/25/Programming-First-Principles-Thinking.html) — refusing to solve a problem with the first niggly fix that comes to mind, and instead asking what the smallest true thing underneath it actually is, *for the regime you're actually in*.

In my first physics course at university, before we were allowed to solve a single problem, we were taught to invent notations for the problem domain — essentially small, purpose-built DSLs — and only then start reasoning inside them. That's `>>` analysis again, just applied to notation instead of forces: decide what regime you're in, build the smallest language that's valid there, and don't pretend it generalizes further than it does.

I don't have a tidy answer for how you teach that skill. Pattern-matching a problem to its right regime seems to take taste as much as method. But I'm fairly sure teaching it starts by refusing the premise I opened with — that one notation, sufficiently general, could ever replace it.
