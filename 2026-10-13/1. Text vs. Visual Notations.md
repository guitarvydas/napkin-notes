# Text vs. Visual Notations
2026-10-13

Some say that "text is more powerful than visual". 

I feel that this statement needs to be further qualified with the question "For what purpose?".

Off the top of my head, I see several various purposes:
- human -> formal analysis
- human -> hardware control
- human -> human communication

Because text supports recursion and higher order abstractions (closures, generics) in a small amount of space, I would consider that a very restricted form of text is "more powerful" for human->formal analysis purposes.

Hardware CPUs are based on the principles of mutation and control flow (aka sequencing and GOTO). Hardware CPUs are little sequencers / interpreters and are programmed with binary codes, often written as assembler text. Here, text, again is quite suitable for human->control usage, but not the same kind of text as used for the above formal analysis, since modern formal forms explicitly deny the existence of mutation and control flow - the basics of hardware CPUs. In fact, functional text notation needs to have warts added to it, that break the basic tenets of structured programming (1-in 1-out) with unruly, dynamic control flow constructs like "exceptions".

For the purposes of human->human communication, visual is clearly "better". Most real engineering disciplines use diagrams, drawings, and blueprints for human-to-human communication. Many humans, especially non-programmers and non-language-analysts want "simplicity" and a layered approach to understanding the details of any system or dataset. Simplicity is "the lack of nuance", hence, the textual notation used for detailed formal analysis is unsuitable for this kind of communication. It is difficult to clearly express[^hard] common design constructs in text, like feedback (which is very different from recursion), looping, multi-path control flows, asynchronous concurrency, etc.

The formal concept of "functions" as the only encapsulation mechanism is a poor substitute for "not colouring outside of the lines of closed figures" that we all learned in kindergarten. The concept of "global variables", "free variables" rose out of the closed-figure inadequacies of textual notation.

[^hard]: It is not necessarily impossible to express these concepts in text, but it is difficult to do so in a way that immediately conveys meaning to other humans. Aside: asynchronous concurrency (parallelism) is not at all expressible in a sequential, synchronous form - the best one can do is to simulate a small subset of synchronous (the exact opposite of "asynchronous") cases of memory-cell-level-synchronized instances of such behaviours.
## Isomorphism - Can We Convert Between Visual and Text?

Can we freely convert between visual and text?

Yes.

Modern diagram editors, like [drawio](drawio.com),  [excalidraw](excalidraw.com), [yed](https://www.yworks.com/products/yed), etc. save diagrams out in XML (graphML, SVG) format, which is a text format. 

That XML-ish format is difficult to read for human eyes, but, is easily parseable by automated tools.

My favourite tool for parsing such formats is based on [PEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsing_expression_grammar) technology, such as [OhmJS](ohmjs.org).

I use a 2-stage process to convert diagrams to running code
1. parse XML-ish
2. rewrite from XML-ish to different text (say, to Python code).

In fact, I've created a little language (a DSL or SCN (Solution Centric Notation), I call RWR, to supplement pass 2 of the above process and combine OhmJS and RWR into a wrapper tool that I call T2T that makes the 2-stage transmogrification process convenient to use.
