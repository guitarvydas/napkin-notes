# TDPL, BUPL, and the Trade-Off We Never Revisited
2026-08-31

There's an old, slightly obscure acronym worth resurrecting: **TDPL**, "Top-Down Parsing Language." It was coined by Aho and Ullman in _The Theory of Parsing, Translation and Compiling_, describing formal work Alexander Birman had done a couple years earlier under the name "TMG Schema." TDPL — and its generalization, GTDPL — modeled the kind of backtracking recursive-descent parser that programmers had already been writing by hand.

Most of the parsers we actually learned in school, though, came from the other direction. YACC and its descendants are bottom-up. There's no equally catchy acronym for that lineage, so let's coin one to match: **BUPL**, "Bottom-Up Parsing Language." It's a fair pairing, because the two approaches aren't just different algorithms — they carve up the space of expressible languages differently. BUPL-style parsers are built to handle context-free grammars. TDPL-style parsers, as it turns out, are strictly more powerful: GTDPL can recognize some languages that aren't context-free at all.

That gap is the part I want to dwell on.

Context-free grammars were never chosen because they were the natural shape of the languages we wanted to describe. They were chosen because they were the shape we could _analyze_ — tractably, on 1960s and 70s hardware, with the theory available at the time. CFGs are a technology-driven compromise, not a human-driven one. The rules people actually want to express — "this identifier must already be declared," "indentation determines scope," "this word means something different depending on what came before it" — are context-sensitive by nature. We just got very good at designing around that fact, and then forgot it was a workaround.

TDPL-style parsing didn't disappear; it resurfaced as **packrat parsing** and PEG (parsing expression grammar) technology. Packrat parsers take the old backtracking recursive-descent idea and make it linear-time by memoizing every partial match. The result is a parser that can express things a CFG struggles with — longest-match rules, syntactic predicates, lookahead-sensitive grammar — in a single, readable grammar, without the contortions BUPL grammars require to fake context sensitivity.

The reason packrat parsing lost the popularity contest for so long comes down to one word: _slow_. Naive backtracking parsing can blow up combinatorially, and memoizing every rule at every position isn't free — it trades time for space, aggressively. In the 20th century, that trade was a real cost. Memory was precious; cycles were precious. "Slow" meant something concrete and often disqualifying.

But "slow" in the 21st century is a different animal. We routinely throw gigabytes of RAM and billions of cycles per second at problems that would have been unthinkable to solve this way fifty years ago. A packrat parser's memory appetite, once a dealbreaker, is now a rounding error on a phone. The trade-off that justified CFGs and bottom-up parsing was a trade-off against _scarcity_. We're not scarce anymore, at least not in the way that mattered when the decision was made.

So it's worth asking, plainly: if the constraint that shaped our parsing technology has largely dissolved, why are we still defaulting to the grammar formalism that constraint produced? Context-sensitive, TDPL-style, packrat-flavored parsing isn't a curiosity from before the field "matured" — it may be the more honest match for how humans actually express structure. We just needed the hardware to catch up to the idea. It has.
