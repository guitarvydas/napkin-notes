How can one teach how to create designs that side-step the general proof machinery?

Provability is a detriment in the early stages of design, whereas it is a necessity in the late stages of production engineering.

---

You've actually just extended your own Design Engineering / Production Engineering split into a place it hadn't explicitly gone yet, and it holds up. Let me sharpen the mechanism, because I think it explains _why_ provability is specifically the detriment, not just that it happens to correlate with the wrong phase.

## Why provability is early-phase poison, mechanically

A proof isn't neutral — it requires you to first commit to the exact boundary you're proving something about. To prove "this type never needs more than 65,535 entries" you must first decide that 65,535 is the boundary. That decision, once made structural (baked into a schema, an API, a type signature that other code starts depending on), resists revision — not for psychological reasons but for load-bearing ones: every caller that starts relying on the u16 index becomes a thing you'd have to touch to widen it later. So a proof made early doesn't just fail to help exploration — it actively punishes the exploration that comes after it, by making the wrong boundary expensive to walk back from. That's the actual content of "premature rigor breaks the design phase": it's not that types are bad, it's that a committed boundary is a one-way door, and you don't yet know which side of the door you want to be on.

Worth separating out, though: the detriment is specifically _structural_ proof — the kind that becomes an interface other code depends on. A scratch assertion in a REPL, a quick sanity check you throw away in an hour, costs nothing and can even help exploration by catching a wrong assumption early. The failure mode isn't "checking things," it's "checking things in a form that calcifies."

## The design skill, named precisely

What you're describing teaching isn't "know more type theory" — it's a specific perceptual habit: **finding the boundary the domain actually has, from evidence, before deciding whether it's worth closing.** That's a different skill than either extreme people default to: reflexively generalizing ("what if someday we need Unicode-32 strings" — closing nothing, out of fear) or reflexively narrowing ("obviously nobody needs more than 255 entries" — closing something you haven't actually observed). Both are premature; the second one just looks like discipline while committing the identical sin your thesis is about — deciding the boundary before the evidence is in.

## How I'd actually try to teach it

A few exercises that train the habit directly, rather than stating it as a principle to remember:

1. **Count the cases before you generalize.** Before designing any interface, pull real examples — actual logs, actual data, actual prior usage — and ask what the _observed_ distribution of cases looks like. This is literally profiling, aimed at design instead of runtime: you don't optimize the 95% case by guessing which 5% is hot, you measure it. Most "what if" generalizations in a spec turn out, on inspection, to defend against a case that has never once occurred in the actual data. That's the exercise: go find out, don't imagine.
    
2. **Force an explicit closed/open label on every field.** For each piece of a design, make the designer write down "closed — I have observed the entire range, it is X" or "open — I cannot bound this yet." This alone surfaces most premature generality, because "open" written down next to a field that's never actually varied in three years of production data looks obviously wrong once it's visible, in a way it never does while sitting implicit in someone's head as habitual caution.
    
3. **Study exemplars as case law, not principles.** SSO in C++/Swift, UTF-8's ASCII-compatible design, Unix's "everything is a byte stream" — each one found a narrow, closed, provable common case and gave it a dedicated cheap path, with a separate, explicit, more expensive escape hatch for the genuinely open remainder. Reading these as worked examples of _where the line got drawn and why_ teaches the pattern-matching skill much faster than being told "prefer closed types" in the abstract.
    
4. **Write it twice, on purpose.** Build the narrowest possible version first — the one that only handles the cases actually in front of you — with the proof machinery entirely absent. Only widen a dimension once a _real, specific, currently-failing case_ demands it, never in anticipation of one. This is Brooks' "build one to throw away" pointed specifically at scope-closing rather than at architecture generally: the first pass's job is to discover where the domain's real edges are, not to survive contact with hypothetical future requirements.
    
5. **Make "hard to prove" a design signal, not a cue to add machinery.** This is the two-pass workflow your split implies directly: Design phase closes the boundary from evidence, with proof deferred entirely. Production phase then goes looking for the proof — types, invariants, tests — and when the proof turns out to be awkward or convoluted, that's not a sign you need a cleverer type system, it's a sign the _design phase drew the boundary in the wrong place_ and you should go back and redraw it, not paper over the seam with more verification machinery. Most teams do the opposite reflexively: awkward-to-prove code gets more annotations and more special cases bolted onto the existing boundary, which is exactly how you end up with the header-and-tag bloat this whole conversation has been measuring — proof machinery accreting around a boundary that was never actually the right one.
    

## Tying back to the 5% problem

The failure mode in the other direction is worth naming explicitly, because it's the same mistake wearing different clothes: assuming _in advance_ which 5% of your domain deserves the closed, provable, zero-overhead treatment, without having measured it. The string pool earned its closed representation because the actual observed need — intern once, read many, never mutate, bounded count, fixed encoding — was stable and known before the representation was committed. If that boundary had been guessed rather than observed, narrowing early would be exactly as premature as generalizing early; it would just look more sophisticated while you did it.
