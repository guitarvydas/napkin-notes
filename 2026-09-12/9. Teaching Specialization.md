Yes — and naming it as generalization-vs-specialization makes the five-decade pattern legible as something other than fashion: it was a rational response to a cost structure that has since changed underneath the pedagogy, which never got updated.

## Why 5 decades of generalization was a reasonable bet, not a mistake

Generalization is a hedge against the cost of being wrong. If undoing a commitment is expensive, the rational strategy is to avoid committing — keep options open, program to an interface, support cases you don't yet need. For most of the last five decades, undoing was genuinely expensive: software shipped on physical media or as a one-time install, a mainframe deployment cycle was measured in months, a published wire format or file format had to stay readable for decades, a COBOL system was written to run essentially unchanged for thirty years. Under those constraints, over-generalizing was cheap insurance and under-generalizing was a decade-long liability. The pedagogy that crystallized out of that era — program to an interface not an implementation, design patterns, SOLID — is a compressed, decontextualized memory of a real cost structure, taught now as timeless principle to people operating under a completely different one.

That cost structure has largely inverted. Git makes any commit reversible in seconds. Feature flags make a bad decision retractable without a redeploy. CI/CD makes shipping and un-shipping cheap and frequent rather than rare and terrifying. The tools for cheap backtracking already exist, broadly deployed — what hasn't caught up is the instinct, because the instinct was never taught as a response to cost, it was taught as virtue. That's the actual gap: not "people don't know how to specialize," but "the curriculum never named the variable — cost of reversal — that should decide whether generalizing or specializing is correct in a given spot," so people default to the inherited habit regardless of whether the constraint that justified it is still present.

## What backtracking actually means here, precisely

It's worth taking the algorithmic sense of the word literally rather than as a metaphor, because it gives you something teachable. A backtracking search — the real technique from N-queens and SAT solvers — commits to a choice, explores forward, detects failure, undoes the _minimal_ state needed to reach the last decision point, and tries an alternative. It only works as a strategy if undoing is cheap; if every commitment required rebuilding the whole search from scratch, nobody would call it "backtracking search," they'd call it "restart from zero," and nobody would use it. That's the exact failure mode of specialization taught without its counterpart: a design decision made without a cheap undo path isn't a specialization, it's a one-way commitment dressed up as one, and treating it that way is what makes people flinch back toward generalizing everything defensively.

So "teach backtracking" really means: **teach people to look at cost-of-reversal as a first-class, measurable property of a boundary, before deciding whether to specialize there.**

## How to actually teach that

1. **Make "cost of undo" an explicit question asked of every commitment, not a background feeling.** For any design decision, have the student answer concretely: how many call sites would need to change, is this contract visible outside this module or this team, is there persisted data whose shape this decision affects. Most people's fear of specializing is calibrated on imagined catastrophe rather than a measured blast radius — asking the question out loud usually reveals the radius is much smaller than the felt aversion, for the overwhelming majority of decisions in a system. Only a genuine minority of boundaries — public APIs, wire formats, schemas with production data behind them, hardware ABIs — actually carry high reversal cost. That's the 5% problem again, now applied to decisions rather than to code paths: most of what people generalize defensively was never expensive to get wrong.
    
2. **Drill the backtrack itself, not just the commitment.** Assign a narrow, specific design. Have students build against it. Then hand them a new requirement engineered to break it, and make them actually perform the reversal — go back, change what needs changing, and _measure_ what that cost. This is exposure therapy for the sunk-cost instinct: most people have never actually practiced discarding a wrong decision on purpose, so they've never calibrated how survivable it is. The lesson isn't "specializing is safe" in the abstract, it's "here is what it concretely cost you last time, and it was smaller than you budgeted for."
    
3. **Teach "spike and delete" as a named, legitimate technique, not an embarrassment.** Build the narrow, possibly-wrong version explicitly labeled as disposable from the outset. Declaring it throwaway _before_ writing it removes the sunk-cost trap that makes people defend a bad specialization once they've invested in it — the commitment to delete was made up front, alongside the commitment to build.
    
4. **Use version control as the literal backtracking mechanism, and teach commit/branch granularity accordingly.** A branch is a search-tree node; a revert is the undo operator. Small, independently revertable commits are what make backtracking a five-minute operation instead of an all-or-nothing gamble — and bundling many specialization decisions into one large commit is what makes reversal feel catastrophic even when any single decision inside it wasn't. This reframes a version-control habit most people already have as the direct practical implementation of the algorithmic idea, rather than two unrelated topics.
    
5. **Study the boundaries that were specifically engineered to make backtracking cheap, as case law.** Protobuf's numbered, optional fields exist so a wire format can be extended later without breaking old readers — that's specialization with a pre-built undo path baked into the format itself, rather than either full commitment or defensive generality. Contrast this against a shared database underlying several services: that boundary makes any one service's schema decision expensive to reverse for everyone, which is why it reliably produces exactly the defensive generalization this whole conversation has been diagnosing. Teaching people to read a design and ask "which kind of boundary is this" is a transferable skill in a way that "prefer loose coupling" as a slogan never quite becomes.
    

The throughline across all five: stop teaching generalization and specialization as competing virtues, and start teaching cost-of-reversal as the one variable that actually decides which is correct in a given spot. Five decades of curriculum taught the conclusion that was right for an environment where reversal was almost always expensive. What's missing isn't a new conclusion to replace it with — it's the measurement that was supposed to precede the conclusion in the first place.
