Agreed with your premise, and I'd push it slightly further: the mistake isn't just twisting a problem to fit a paradigm — it's forgetting that a paradigm is an answer to a _specific_ engineering problem, not a description of computation in general. Once you know what each one was actually built to solve, "does this paradigm fit my problem" becomes a concrete question with a concrete answer, rather than a matter of taste.

## What functional programming is for

FP's central move is treating computation as evaluation of mathematical functions and minimizing (or eliminating) mutable state. That move exists to buy specific things:

- **Equational reasoning.** If a function's output depends only on its inputs, you can substitute a call with its result anywhere, reason about code the way you reason about algebra, and refactor without fear of hidden interactions. This is the actual payoff, not "purity for its own sake."
- **Safe composition.** Pure functions compose (`f . g`) without needing to know each other's internal state — you can build complex behavior from small, independently-verifiable pieces.
- **Fearless concurrency.** No shared mutable state means many operations can run in parallel with no locks, no races, no ordering bugs. This is the practical reason FP surged back into relevance once multicore hardware made shared-mutable-state concurrency a liability rather than a convenience.
- **Compiler leverage.** Purity gives the compiler strong guarantees about when it's safe to reorder, cache, deduplicate, or skip a computation entirely — laziness, fusion, and memoization all trade on exactly the property purity provides.

FP's native abstraction is _transformation_: data goes in, data comes out, nothing in between has identity or a lifetime you need to track. That's why it's a superb fit for compilers, parsers, symbolic math, and data pipelines — problems that genuinely are "compute a new value from old values" — and a poor fit for anything whose actual subject matter is _persistent, mutating identity_. Forcing a domain like that into purity means threading state through every call by hand (monads, zippers, whatever the language offers), which is real, avoidable cost for a problem that was never shaped like a transformation to begin with. That's the mechanism behind your opening sentence: the friction you feel when "twisting" a problem into FP isn't aesthetic, it's the cost of building a fake absence-of-state on top of a domain whose whole point is state.

## What object-oriented programming is for

OOP's central move is bundling state together with the operations that act on it, giving that bundle identity, and hiding its internals behind a stable interface. It answers different problems:

- **Encapsulation as complexity management.** This traces directly to Parnas's information-hiding principle: a module's internals can change freely as long as its interface doesn't, which is what lets large systems evolve without everything breaking at once. This is arguably OOP's real justification, independent of anything else associated with it.
- **Modeling things that actually have identity and change over time.** A bank account, a widget, a network connection, a game entity — these aren't transformations of data, they're _things_ that persist and mutate. OOP's fit here isn't incidental: OOP's ancestor, Simula, was built specifically for discrete-event simulation, where "an object" is literally a simulated entity with its own state. That lineage explains why OOP still dominates GUIs, simulations, and entity-heavy domains — the paradigm's native abstraction matches the domain's actual shape.
- **Polymorphism over an interface you haven't fully enumerated yet.** Writing code against an abstract type and letting concrete behavior be supplied later (including by code that doesn't exist yet — plugins, extensions) is a genuinely hard problem, and dynamic dispatch is a direct, effective answer to it.

Worth separating from all of that: **inheritance-as-code-reuse** is not the core purpose, it's a specific, often-regretted implementation choice. Deep inheritance hierarchies built to avoid duplicating code produce exactly the fragile-base-class problems OOP's critics point at, and most experienced OOP practice has moved toward composition for that reason — but that's a critique of one mechanism, not of encapsulation-plus-dynamic-dispatch itself. It's also worth knowing that Alan Kay's original conception of "object-oriented" was about message-passing, encapsulation, and late binding — closer in spirit to independent communicating components than to the classes-and-inheritance package most languages actually shipped under that name. The version that won out is one specific interpretation, not the only coherent one.

## The synthesis

Both paradigms are answers to different questions:

- FP asks: _how do I reason safely about a computation and combine pieces of it without hidden interaction?_
- OOP asks: _how do I manage a system made of many independent, stateful, interacting things without every part needing to know every other part's internals?_

Neither is "what computation really is" — they're both decompositions imposed on a problem, and the actual engineering skill is recognizing which decomposition matches your domain's native shape before you commit to one. Most real systems, honestly, are a poor fit for a single paradigm applied uniformly top to bottom: the parts of a system that are genuinely about transformation (parsing input, computing a result, validating data) tend to get worse, not better, when forced into objects with mutable internal state; the parts that are genuinely about persistent, interacting entities tend to get worse, not better, when forced into pure functions threading state through every call. The paradigm mismatch cost is the same phenomenon in both directions — it's just more visible when the fashionable direction (FP, lately) is the one being over-applied.
