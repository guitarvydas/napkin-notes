Let me pull apart "sidestepped" carefully, because the word is doing real work — it's not that the pool found a shortcut to the same proof, it's that it made the proof structurally unnecessary, and that distinction is the whole answer.

## How the sidestep actually works

Escape analysis exists to answer a question: _"Does this particular value need the general machinery, or can I skip it here?"_ That question only exists because the general machinery is the language's **default** — every value is assumed heap-allocated, GC-tracked, aliasable, and polymorphic until proven otherwise, one call site at a time. The proof is retroactive: the optimizer is trying to claw back cost from a promise the language already made universally, for the one specific case where the promise turns out to be unneeded.

The pool never made that promise. There's no polymorphism to disprove per-instance because the schema declared, once, that a "string" _is_ a byte-span descriptor into a shared block — full stop, no other shape exists anywhere in the system. Go's compiler has to _discover_, fresh, for every allocation site, whether this particular string is the narrow case or the general case. The pool doesn't discover anything, because it deleted the general case from the space of possibilities before the question could even be asked.

That's the mechanical difference between **proving** something and **declaring** it: proving is a cost paid per-instance, potentially forever, re-derived every compile or every JIT run, because the space of possibilities was left open. Declaring is a cost paid exactly once, at design time, and it's permanent because the space of possibilities was closed.

## The underlying principle

I'd state it like this: **a type or schema is a proof, cached.** When you write `descriptor :: {length: u16, offset: u32}` and mean it totally — not "usually," not "unless someone needs more" — you've discharged a proof obligation once, as the author, and every downstream consumer (every caller, every compiler pass, every future reader) gets to reuse it for free instead of re-deriving it. A dynamically-typed value doesn't get this discount: the question "what could this actually be, right now" has to be re-asked at every single use site, because the language kept it open.

This is also, seen from another angle, the deep argument for static typing generally — not "it catches bugs," but "it caches proofs so no one downstream has to redo the work." Rust's ownership system is the most aggressive modern instance: memory safety is proven once, at compile time, per piece of code, and then never re-derived by a GC at runtime for the rest of the program's life. Monomorphized generics are the same move applied to polymorphism — resolve it once per concrete type at compile time, bake the answer into the binary, rather than deferring it to type-erased boxing that pays a tag-check on every access forever.

The pool is this same move, taken further than Rust bothers to for its own `String`: it doesn't just resolve polymorphism once per type, it deletes the polymorphism from the domain entirely. There is no "kind" of string the descriptor doesn't handle, so there's no dispatch, no tag, no fallback path — and nothing to prove, because proof is only needed where an alternative was still conceivable.

## Can we generally stop building proof machinery this way?

Partially, and the honest boundary matters more than the yes/no.

**Where it works cleanly:** infrastructure-level problems with a genuinely closed, well-understood shape — symbol tables, wire formats, parser output, string interning, ABI boundaries. These are domains where you, the designer, actually know the full space of cases in advance, and "make the illegal states unrepresentable" is a real, available move rather than wishful thinking. This is where narrowing the design pays off with essentially no downside.

**Where it doesn't:** anywhere the space of cases is genuinely open and you don't get to know it in advance — plugin systems, user-supplied code, schemas that must evolve after deployment, anything touching values a language considers "any type." There, closing the representation isn't a design choice you're failing to make out of laziness; it's not available, because the openness is the actual requirement. If you force a closed representation onto a genuinely open problem, you haven't eliminated the proof burden — you've just deferred it to the day someone needs the case you didn't allow for, at which point the cost isn't "re-run an analysis," it's "redesign the format." Closed-world design isn't free of risk; it trades a small recurring tax (runtime headers, GC, dynamic dispatch) for a large, lumpy one (migration cost, paid only if you guessed wrong about the domain's shape).

**The synthesis that actually shows up everywhere once you look for it** — and it's been sitting in this conversation's own data the whole time — is a third option, distinct from both "prove it's safe" and "declare the general case away": **partition the space into a closed common case and an open rare case, and route between them with one cheap tag check.** That's exactly what C++'s and Swift's small-string optimization are doing: the inline buffer is a closed, narrow, zero-proof-needed representation for the case that's true almost always, and the heap fallback is the fully general, fully expensive escape hatch for the rare case that doesn't fit — with a single bit deciding which path you're on. Nobody had to prove the short string doesn't escape or doesn't need capacity tracking; the type itself only offers that guarantee within its 15-byte window, and steps outside it explicitly, on purpose, only when the guarantee genuinely can't hold.

So the sharper version of your question isn't "should we be more careful at design time instead of building proof generators" — it's "which parts of this problem are actually closed, and can I give the closed part a representation so narrow it needs no proof, while confining the genuinely open part to its own explicit, separately-taxed path?" Most systems have more closed structure than their implementations admit; the header you're paying on every string in Python or the JVM isn't there because strings need it 90% of the time — it's there because the language committed to one uniform representation for _all_ values, closed and open alike, and never built the seam that would let the common case opt out.
