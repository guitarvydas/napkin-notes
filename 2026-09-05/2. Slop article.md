# AI Slop vs. Artistry
2026-09-05

I've heard this song before.

Not the current hype about AI, exactly — but the pattern underneath it. I've heard it, watched it, lived through it, in a completely different field.

Years ago I was deeply into songwriting and music production. I watched drum machines, DAWs, auto-tune, and music video technology arrive one after another. Each time, the same thing happened: suddenly, a lot more people could make music. And most of what they made sounded like slop — thin, degraded versions of the real, artistic thing.

It wasn't the technology's fault. The slop came from the masses playing with a new medium and stopping at the first thing it handed them. Push a button, get a beat, call it done. The technology lowered the floor, and most people stood exactly on it.

But not everyone stopped there. Some actual artists sat with these same tools long enough to learn them — not just operate them, but _work_ them, push against their grain, find out what they could do that nothing before them could do. Once that happened, the results stopped sounding like slop. The drum machine that produced a million disposable beats also produced works of art. Same box. Different relationship to it.

I think we're in that exact moment with AI.

A lot more people can now produce music, images, and prose that resembles what came before. Most of it is slop, for the same old reason — first draft accepted, first image accepted, first melody accepted. Meanwhile, the "real artists" mostly haven't figured out yet how to manipulate this new medium the way earlier artists eventually learned to manipulate drum machines and DAWs. That reckoning is still ahead of us, not behind us.

Here's the distinction I keep coming back to: artists evoke feelings. Slop generators produce formulaic output that doesn't touch anyone. The difference was never really about the tool. It's about whether a human being with intent is wrestling with the tool, or just accepting whatever the tool coughs up first.

## How I actually use it

I've been using AI — Claude, and Leanpub's ghostAI — to write blog posts, articles, and book chapters for a few years now. I think I'm getting better at it, which is itself a sign that this is a skill and not a shortcut.

What it does well is take my point-form notes and turn them into readable prose with a lot less effort than it used to take me. But that's the small part. The bigger part is that Claude reads what I've written and criticizes it — as a piece of writing, not just as a string of facts. It acts as a brainstorming co-writer. I don't hand it notes and accept whatever comes back. I argue with it. I push on the draft, criticize it, refine it, the way I would with a human co-writer — though, admittedly, with fewer arguments and less ego in the room.

My process usually goes three rounds:

1. I write a set of ideas in point form.
2. I ask Claude to draft an article from them.
3. I read the draft with a note-taking window open beside it, jotting down my reactions line by line — what lands, what doesn't, what's missing, what's wrong. When I hit the end, I paste my notes back in and wait for the revision.

I repeat that loop about three times, until I run out of notes to make. At that point the piece is mine in the way that matters — not because I typed every word, but because every sentence has passed through my judgment at least once.

That loop is the whole game. It's the difference between accepting the first draft and actually working the medium.

## What this would look like for songwriting

If I applied this same process to songwriting, I wouldn't feed a prompt to a model and take whatever came out. I'd want to bring my own craft _into_ the loop.

Years ago I studied lyric writing with Pat Pattison — his structural approach to rhyme, prosody, and the relationship between a lyric's form and its emotional payload. Those aren't vague preferences; they're closer to rules. I'd want to cast those Pattison rules into something like AI "learnings" — a lens the model has to write through, not just a suggestion it can politely ignore. Then I wouldn't stop at the first pass. I'd go around the loop the same way I do with prose: react, push back, ask for variations, notice what rings false against my own ear, and refine.

I'd do the same thing with melody and music — a few passes, treating the AI as a source of inspiration and raw material to react against, not a finish line. The model proposes; I judge; we go again.

## The tool doesn't have the taste

That last part is the whole point. AI should help an artist arrive at their artistic vision _faster_ — not hand them a vision they never had. It's a tool. Nothing more mystical than that. A typewriter didn't write better novels than a quill; it just let a writer who already had something to say get out of their own way faster. A junior co-writer doesn't replace the senior one's taste; they generate raw material and rough drafts for the senior writer to shape.

That's what AI is, in the hands of someone who knows what they're after: a fast, tireless, occasionally brilliant junior collaborator. It has no taste of its own to impose — only the taste you bring to the editing.

The slop isn't a property of the technology. It's what happens when nobody in the loop has taste, and nobody's willing to go around a second time.
