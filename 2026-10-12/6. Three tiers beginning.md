Here is a replacement for the opening, plus a new section on the cottage industry:

> # Three Tiers of Programming a Device
> 
> ## Three Tiers, Not One
> 
> There are (at least) three tiers to programming a device:
> 
> 1. Architecture
> 2. Engineering
> 3. Production
> 
> Architecture decides what the thing is: its overall form, its parts, and how it should feel to the people who use it. This is the clay modeler's work.
> 
> Engineering checks that the architecture holds together. It makes sure every piece has been designed in, that the pieces can be affixed to one another in some manner, and that the i's are dotted and the t's crossed. It does not choose the cheapest glue or fastener for every situation.
> 
> Production takes a settled design and makes it fast, small, cheap, and shippable. This is the stamping die and the fastener catalogue. It is a different kind of engineering, concerned with optimizing along particular dimensions. Beneath it sits the plain act of implementation, which is not engineering at all. It is carrying out a plan that has already been made.
> 
> Architecture and Engineering together are what I have been calling Design. Production is the other half.
> 
> Most of our AOT (Ahead of Time, statically, strongly typed) programming languages are geared for Production. They are excellent at catching mistakes in the details and generating tight machine code.
> 
> ## The Cottage Industry Mentality
> 
> Yet we use the same, single kind of programming language for all of it. Architecture, engineering, and implementation are conflated, and one person, in one language, in one sitting, does all three. This is the cottage industry. The village cabinetmaker sketches the chair, works out the joinery, selects the wood, and cuts it, all at one bench with one set of tools. For one chair this works well. It does not scale to a skyscraper, a car, or a game engine.
> 
> Industries that grew out of the cottage stage did so by finding clean separations between the tiers, and a notation for each one. The architect's drawings are not the engineer's calculations, and neither is the machinist's tooling spec. Each tier has its own notation, tuned to the questions that tier asks, and each hands something well-defined to the next.
> 
> Programming has not made this move. We have one notation, shaped by the needs of the lowest tier, and we ask it to carry the thinking of the other two. To leave the cottage, we need to find the clean separations between architecture, engineering, and production, and a notation for each.

On a better term than "design engineer": since you think of that tier as engineering proper, the plain word "Engineering" may be enough, with "Architecture" above it taking over the design role. If you want something more specific, here are some options:

- **Systems engineering** is an established term for checking that all the pieces fit together, but it carries baggage from large-scale hardware and aerospace.
- **Integration engineering** names the "can it all be affixed" concern directly.
- **Product engineering** matches the automotive usage, with "manufacturing engineering" as the Production counterpart, but in software it means something quite different.
- **Detail design** is the traditional name for this tier in physical-product work, though it sounds less like engineering.
