> was Rebol the first language that incorporated PEG-like language/dialect building?

**No—not if by “PEG-like” you mean the modern Parsing Expression Grammar formalism.** But your intuition about **Rebol being an early and important example of the _language/dialect-building idea_** is quite good.

There are really two histories that converge here.

### Rebol's dialects predate PEG

Rebol appeared in **1997**, and its central idea of _dialects_ was already part of the language. Rebol's `PARSE` provided a grammar-like dialect in which the programmer could construct a little language out of Rebol values:

```rebol
parse input [
    some "abc"
    ...
]
```

Rebol's documentation explicitly describes dialects as **sub-languages of Rebol** that retain the same lexical representation but give the values a different meaning/order. Remarkably, it says that the **PARSE rules themselves are a dialect**. ([rebol.com](https://www.rebol.com/docs/core23/rebolcore-15.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Chapter 15 - Parsing"))

And Carl Sassenrath explicitly described `parse` as using its own grammar and vocabulary—a dialect. ([rebol.com](https://www.rebol.com/faq.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "REBOL Language FAQ"))

### But Rebol's parser wasn't technically PEG

This is the interesting part.

Rebol's `PARSE` is described by Red's documentation as an **embedded DSL based on the TDPL family**. ([red-lang.org](https://www.red-lang.org/2013/11/041-introducing-parse.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Red Programming Language: 0.4.1: Introducing Parse"))

That's very close to PEG conceptually.

In fact, Bryan Ford's 2004 PEG paper says that PEGs are reducible to **TS/TDPL and gTS/GTDPL**, recognition systems developed around **1970**. ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/964001.964011?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Parsing expression grammars | Proceedings of the 31st ACM SIGPLAN-SIGACT symposium on Principles of programming languages"))

So the historical relationship is approximately:

```text
1970s
TDPL / GTDPL
    │
    │
    ├───────────────┐
    │               │
    ▼               ▼
Rebol PARSE       Bryan Ford's PEG
(~1997)              (2004)
    │
    ▼
"dialect" idea
```

Thus **Rebol's `PARSE` was not implementing something invented by PEG**, and PEG wasn't invented until Ford's 2004 paper. ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/964001.964011?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Parsing expression grammars | Proceedings of the 31st ACM SIGPLAN-SIGACT symposium on Principles of programming languages"))

### But Rebol's _dialect_ idea is arguably more interesting

I think this is what you're getting at.

A conventional parser framework says:

> Here's a grammar describing another language.

Rebol says something closer to:

> **Here's a block of values. Interpret that block according to a particular vocabulary and grammar.**

That's a much more **Lisp/Forth-like** approach to language construction.

For example, the same underlying Rebol representation can be interpreted as different dialects:

```text
Rebol values
     │
     ├── normal Rebol
     │
     ├── PARSE dialect
     │
     ├── VID GUI dialect
     │
     ├── other domain dialect
     │
     └── your own dialect
```

The syntax doesn't necessarily change radically. **The interpretation of the values changes.**

That is an important distinction from conventional DSL technology.

### So I would phrase the historical claim this way

If your question is:

> **Was Rebol the first language to have PEGs?**

**No.** PEGs as such came later, in Ford's 2004 work, and their theoretical ancestors go back to the early 1970s. ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/964001.964011?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Parsing expression grammars | Proceedings of the 31st ACM SIGPLAN-SIGACT symposium on Principles of programming languages"))

If your question is:

> **Was Rebol one of the earliest languages to make embedded, user-definable DSLs/dialects a central part of the language?**

**Yes, absolutely.** That's one of Rebol's defining ideas. Its documentation explicitly calls dialects _sub-languages_ and presents `PARSE` itself as a dialect. ([rebol.com](https://www.rebol.com/docs/core23/rebolcore-15.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Chapter 15 - Parsing"))

And there's an especially nice connection to what we've been discussing about **Janet**:

```text
Lisp
   ↓
code is data
   ↓
macros / DSLs

Rebol
   ↓
values are syntax
   ↓
dialects / PARSE
   ↓
embedded DSLs

Janet
   ↓
Lisp-like homoiconicity
   +
PEG
   +
macros
   ↓
embedded DSLs
```

So **Rebol may actually be a more historically relevant predecessor to Janet's "PEG + homoiconic data + dialect/DSL" combination than PEG itself is**. That's a direction I'd be interested in tracing further if you're investigating the lineage of _language-oriented programming_.

> Rebol inspired the invention of JSON.

Yes — and that's a point worth emphasizing. **Douglas Crockford himself explicitly credits Rebol as one of the inspirations for JSON.**

In a recent Q&A, Crockford was asked what inspired JSON. His answer was:

> “There were two brilliant languages, Lisp and Rebol, that had a portable representation of data structures in their syntax.” ([Crockford](https://www.crockford.com/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Questions & Answers: 3"))

He explains the connection more fully in _How JavaScript Works_: Rebol uses its textual representation for both programs and data, and this made it natural to use that representation to **serialize data for transmission**. Crockford recognized that JavaScript's object literals offered a similar possibility. ([Crockford](https://www.crockford.com/image/howjsworks.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "How JavaScript Works"))

So the lineage is roughly:

```text
Lisp ──────────┐
               │
Rebol ─────────┼──→ Crockford's recognition
               │       that JavaScript syntax
JavaScript ────┘       could represent data
                         │
                         ▼
                        JSON
```

There is an important nuance, though: **Rebol did not directly become JSON.** Crockford discovered in 2001 that a subset of JavaScript's object-literal syntax was a convenient data interchange format. He subsequently named it JSON, published it, and promoted it as a standard. He himself says that others had independently used JavaScript literals for data transport as early as 1996. ([JSON](https://www.json.org/fatfree.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "JSON: The Fat-Free Alternative to XML"))

What Rebol contributed was the **conceptual recognition that a programming language's textual syntax can simultaneously be a compact, portable representation of data**.

And Rebol's representation was considerably richer than JSON. For example, Rebol naturally represents dates, URLs, words, blocks, binary values, etc., as typed values rather than reducing everything to strings. Carl Sassenrath himself later contrasted a JSON representation with the corresponding Rebol representation. ([Rebol](https://www.rebol.com/article/0522.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "On JSON and REBOL"))

So your earlier observation about Rebol's **dialects** is part of a larger and rather remarkable design philosophy:

> **Rebol treated syntax, data, and domain-specific languages as different interpretations of a common textual/value representation.**

That makes Rebol much more historically interesting than its current popularity would suggest. It influenced **JSON**, **Red**, and arguably a whole family of ideas around lightweight embedded DSLs and language-oriented programming.

> I want to know more about Rebol and Parse. Can I re-implement it in something more modern, like Python, Janet, Common Lisp?

Yes. **You can reimplement Rebol's `PARSE` quite realistically in Python, Janet, or Common Lisp**, and I think doing so would be an excellent way to understand what Rebol was actually doing.

The key is to separate two things that Rebol combines:

1. **The parsing engine**
    
2. **The dialect that describes the grammar**
    

Rebol's `parse` takes an input series and a **block of values that is itself a grammar**. Rebol calls this a dialect: the same underlying values—strings, words, blocks, integers, etc.—are given specialized meanings by `parse`. ([Rebol](https://www.rebol.com/docs/core23/rebolcore-15.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Chapter 15 - Parsing"))

For example, Rebol can express a grammar approximately like:

```rebol
digits: charset "0123456789"

phone: [
    "(" 3 digits ")"
    3 digits "-" 4 digits
]

parse "(416)555-1234" phone
```

The interesting part isn't really the PEG-like algorithm. **It's that `phone` is a Rebol data structure which is interpreted as a little programming language.**

### Python

You could reproduce this fairly directly:

```python
phone = [
    "(",
    Repeat(3, Digit()),
    ")",
    Repeat(3, Digit()),
    "-",
    Repeat(4, Digit())
]

parse("(416)555-1234", phone)
```

But Python lists aren't particularly interesting as a substrate because you have to invent objects such as `Repeat`, `Digit`, `Choice`, etc.

You could instead make a very literal representation:

```python
phone = [
    "(", ("repeat", 3, "digit"), ")",
    ("repeat", 3, "digit"), "-",
    ("repeat", 4, "digit")
]
```

Then your parser interprets those values.

### Common Lisp

**Common Lisp is much more interesting.**

You could write something resembling Rebol:

```lisp
(defparameter *phone*
  '("(" (repeat 3 digit) ")"
    (repeat 3 digit) "-"
    (repeat 4 digit)))
```

Now you have essentially recreated the important Rebol idea:

```text
Lisp data
    ↓
interpreted as
    ↓
a parsing dialect
```

And Lisp's homoiconicity makes it particularly natural to extend the dialect with macros.

### Janet

**Janet may be the most interesting of the three for this experiment.**

You could write:

```janet
(def phone
  ["(" (repeat 3 digit) ")"
   (repeat 3 digit) "-"
   (repeat 4 digit)])
```

Then implement `parse` to interpret that structure.

But Janet gives you something even more relevant: **PEG is already built into Janet**, so you could compare your Rebol-style interpreter against Janet's native PEG facilities.

### What I'd actually implement

I wouldn't try to reproduce _all_ of Rebol `PARSE` initially. I'd make a tiny **Rebol Parse interpreter** with perhaps 15 operations:

```text
literal        "abc"
sequence       [ ... ]
choice         [ ... | ... ]
any            any rule
some           some rule
opt            opt rule
repeat         N rule
range          N M rule
to             to rule
thru           thru rule
skip           skip
end            end
copy           copy variable rule
set             set variable
into            into rule
```

Those capture a large part of the interesting idea. Rebol's actual `PARSE` has considerably more—including input modification, evaluation of embedded expressions, and parsing of Rebol blocks as well as strings. ([Rebol](https://www.rebol.com/r3/docs/concepts/parsing-summary.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "REBOL 3 Concepts: Parsing: Summary of Parse Operations"))

And then I'd add **one crucial feature**:

```text
(expression)
```

Rebol allows a parenthesized expression in a parse rule to escape the parsing dialect and evaluate ordinary Rebol code. ([Rebol](https://www.rebol.com/r3/docs/concepts/parsing-summary.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "REBOL 3 Concepts: Parsing: Summary of Parse Operations"))

That's where the idea starts becoming much more than "a PEG parser."

### The really interesting experiment

I'd build it in **Janet first**, because you'd get:

```text
                 Janet
                   │
       ┌───────────┴───────────┐
       │                       │
   ordinary Janet        your Parse dialect
       │                       │
       └───────────┬───────────┘
                   │
              Janet data
```

Then you can ask the really interesting question:

> **What is the minimum machinery required to turn an ordinary homoiconic language into a language for creating other languages?**

That's essentially what Rebol was doing.

And Red makes this explicit: its documentation describes Parse as an **embedded DSL for grammar rules**, with ordered alternatives, backtracking, repetition, extraction, modification, and even use in implementing other DSLs. ([GitHub](https://github.com/red/docs/blob/master/en/parse.adoc?utm_source=chatgpt.com "docs/en/parse.adoc at master · red/docs · GitHub"))

So I wouldn't approach this as "implement a PEG parser in Python." I'd approach it as:

> **Implement Rebol's idea of a parsing dialect.**

That's a much more interesting project—and it would let you experiment with the part of Rebol that is most relevant to your interest in **DSLs, homoiconicity and diagrammatic languages**.
